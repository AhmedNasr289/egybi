One hundred and sixteen million, mostly on a ribbon of green

Egypt had about 27 million people in 1960. It now has around 116 million. But the more striking number is how few square kilometres almost all of them actually live on.

Egypt’s population has more than quadrupled in a lifetime: roughly 27 million in 1960, about 58 million by 1990, and around 116 million by 2024 (World Bank). That is one of the largest and fastest population expansions of any major country in the period.

The headline everyone quotes is the total. The number that actually explains the country is where those people are. The vast majority of Egyptians live on the Nile valley and delta — a thin green ribbon that is only a small fraction of the national territory. The rest is desert. So a country that looks enormous on a map behaves, in daily terms, like a very crowded strip along a single river.

That concentration is why Cairo and Giza feel the way they do, why the government has spent a decade trying to build new cities out in the desert to relieve the valley, and why almost every big national project — new capital, new farmland, desalination, high-speed rail — is in some sense an attempt to give a growing population somewhere else to go.

It is also a young country: a large share of Egyptians are under 30. That is a challenge (jobs, housing, schools) and an asset (a big workforce, a huge domestic market) at the same time — the demographic version of the currency story, painful and promising in the same breath.
