Data stories

Egypt, argued from the map.

Three stories where the map moves as you read. Every figure below is computed in your browser from the data in this repository, so the numbers in the prose cannot drift away from their source. Scroll, or press Tab to walk the steps by keyboard.

For a first visit

Half of Egypt lives on a fortieth of it.

The country is enormous and almost all of it is empty. Here is what that means for where you will actually go.

Egypt is mostly empty

Egypt covers — square kilometres and holds — people. Both numbers are true, and together they mislead: the population is not spread across the country, it is pressed into a thin strip beside one river and one delta.

Half the country, on a rounding error of land

Rank the governorates by density and add them up. Half of everyone in Egypt lives in the — densest, which together are — of the land area.

That is the Nile valley and the Delta, in blue. If you visit Egypt, almost everything you will see is inside this shape.

And the rest

Now the opposite end. — governorates hold under 25 people per square kilometre. They are — of Egypt's land and — of its people.

This is the Western Desert, Sinai and the Red Sea coast — in gold. Larger than most European countries, and nearly nobody lives there.

The shape of the country

Put them together and Egypt's real geography appears: a blue ribbon of people, a gold expanse of almost nobody, and very little in between.

Every road, every city and nearly every ancient site in this encyclopaedia sits on the blue. That is not a metaphor — it is what the density data says.

Sources. Governorate boundaries: geoBoundaries EGY ADM1 (CC BY 4.0). Population, area and density attributes: CAPMAS figures bundled with those boundaries. Percentages are computed in the browser from those attributes. What this cannot tell you: governorate averages hide everything inside them — Giza spans both dense city and open desert, and a single density figure flattens that.

For a resident

This is not a map of hospitals.

It is a map of where people have done the mapping — and telling the difference is the most useful skill for reading any open dataset about Egypt.

A map of hospitals, or a map of mappers?

The engineering

Every planning era leaves a fingerprint you can measure.

Street geometry is one of the few things about a city you can measure objectively from open data. Here is what a century of Egyptian plans looks like in numbers.

A century of plans, measured

EgyBI measures the street geometry of — Egyptian districts spanning the 1860s to the 2020s, from OpenStreetMap on —. Intersection density, dead-end share, circuity and orientation entropy are computed the way a highway engineer would, then normalised per square kilometre.

The oldest fabric

Downtown Cairo, laid out for Khedive Ismail in the 1860s, is the densest network measured here. Short blocks, many junctions, walkable by construction rather than by policy.

Its boundary is the one hand-drawn extent on the site, because OpenStreetMap has no polygon for Downtown Cairo at all — and it is labelled as hand-drawn everywhere it appears.

The newest

The desert cities invert it. Longer segments, fewer junctions per square kilometre, higher circuity — the distance you actually travel versus the straight line.

What the geometry cannot tell you

None of this says which district is better to live in. A cul-de-sac layout is quieter on your own street and harder to cross on foot; both are true at once, and the geometry measures the trade-off without resolving it.

It also says nothing about pavement quality, traffic, safety or whether the street is pleasant. Those are not in the data, so they are not in the conclusion.
