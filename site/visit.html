Plan check

For a first visit

What Egypt is really like, month by month.

Where the great sites actually are, how high above the sea they sit, and what each month is really like at each one — from NASA’s climate records and a radar survey of the land. We will not tell you the “best time to visit”; we show you the numbers so you can decide how much heat suits you.

Orientation

Almost nobody visits most of Egypt.

Egypt is about a million square kilometres and a visitor sees a thread of it. Scroll, and the map follows.

One country, two habitable strips

Egypt covers roughly a million square kilometres, and almost all of it is desert nobody lives in. The inhabited country is the Nile and its delta — a green thread and a fan — plus two coasts. Every site below sits on one of them.

The Delta and the capital

The Nile splits north of Cairo and fans into the Mediterranean. This is the densest, wettest, mildest part of the country: Cairo, Alexandria, the pyramid fields at Giza and Saqqara on the desert edge just west of the river. If you have three days, they are here.

Upper Egypt, which is in the south

Confusingly, Upper Egypt is the southern half — upper as in upstream. The Nile runs north, so everything is named from the water’s point of view. Luxor sits here, with Karnak on the east bank and the Valley of the Kings on the west. It is markedly hotter than Cairo, and the table below says by how much.

The far south

Aswan and, 280 km further on, Abu Simbel — close enough to Sudan that the temples were cut apart and lifted 65 metres in the 1960s to escape the rising lake behind the High Dam. The hottest readings on this page are here.

The Western Desert

West of the river is the Sahara, punctuated by oases. Siwa is close to the Libyan border and was isolated enough to keep its own Berber language. The White Desert is chalk eroded into shapes. Both are a long drive from anywhere, which is the point of them.

Sinai and the Red Sea

The peninsula between Africa and Asia: mountains, Saint Catherine’s monastery at the foot of one of them, and reef coast at Dahab. Saint Catherine sits high enough to be genuinely cold in winter — the only place on this page where that is true.

Heat is the thing to plan around

What these numbers are not. They are long-run monthly averages from NASA’s POWER climate data, built on the MERRA-2 reanalysis — not weather-station readings, and not a forecast. They answer “what is August usually like at Abu Simbel”. They cannot tell you about the day you actually travel, and a single hot week will not show up in them.

Three figures, three different questions. The day and night columns are the average daily high and low for each month across 2001–2020, from POWER’s monthly series — what a normal afternoon and night actually reach. Each site’s own panel also shows the all-day average and the record extremes, the hottest and coldest hours ever measured in that month. An earlier version of this page showed those extremes labelled “mean daily maximum”, which put Giza’s August at 45 °C when a normal afternoon is nearer 35. POWER’s long-term averages cannot give a daily high at all, but its monthly series can, so the page now uses both.

Measured by radar

Why there is no 3D flythrough. A cinematic aerial tour of Egypt would need either footage somebody filmed, which we do not have, or computer-generated imagery, which would put made-up terrain next to measured figures on the same page. So instead you get the real ground height at each site’s own coordinates, from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission’s 30-metre survey. Every bar is a radar measurement of real land.

The ground, measured along a line

Walk the land in cross-section.

An elevation for a single point tells you almost nothing. What explains Egypt is the shape of the ground — how narrow the green strip really is, that the pyramids stand on a scarp their builders chose rather than raised, how far the desert falls below the sea. Each line below is sampled from NASA’s radar elevation model at up to two hundred points. Every metre is a measurement of the land, and none of it is an artist’s impression.

How we check the numbers

Every temperature above was tested against a weather station.

The figures on this page come from a model, not a thermometer. So before we publish them, each one is compared with the official averages the Egyptian Meteorological Authority publishes through the World Meteorological Organization — a completely separate source, so a mistake in reading the first cannot slip through. If the comparison fails, nothing is published. Here is that comparison in full, including where the two disagree.

Where all of this came from

Sources, and what they cannot tell you.

Loading source information…

We do not describe how each place feels, and that is on purpose. No sourced dataset can say what Karnak feels like at dawn, and writing it ourselves would put made-up atmosphere beside measured temperatures, where you could reasonably take it to be equally sourced. So each place shows what the data holds — where it is, how high, how hot each month, whether UNESCO lists it — and stops there.

Nothing here is a recommendation. “The best time to visit” depends on your tolerance for heat, your budget and how much you mind crowds, none of which are in any dataset. The measurement is shown; the judgement is yours.

The list of sites is ours. These — places were chosen as the ones a first-time visitor is realistically choosing between, which is an editorial call, not a ranking derived from data. Egypt has hundreds of significant sites; the gazetteer holds every one OpenStreetMap has mapped.
