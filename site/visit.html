For a first visit

Egypt, measured rather than marketed.

Where the great sites actually are, how high above the sea they sit, and what each month is really like at each one — from NASA’s climate records and a radar survey of the land. No adjectives, no “best time to visit”: the measurements, and your own judgement about the heat.

Orientation

Almost nobody visits most of Egypt.

Egypt is about a million square kilometres and a visitor sees a thread of it. Scroll, and the map follows.

One country, two habitable strips

Egypt covers roughly a million square kilometres, and almost all of it is desert nobody lives in. The inhabited country is the Nile and its delta — a green thread and a fan — plus two coasts. Every site below sits on one of them.

The Delta and the capital

The Nile splits north of Cairo and fans into the Mediterranean. This is the densest, wettest, mildest part of the country: Cairo, Alexandria, the pyramid fields at Giza and Saqqara on the desert edge just west of the river. If you have three days, they are here.

Upper Egypt, which is in the south

Confusingly, Upper Egypt is the southern half — upper as in upstream. The Nile runs north, so everything is named from the water’s point of view. Luxor sits here, with Karnak on the east bank and the Valley of the Kings on the west. It is markedly hotter than Cairo, and the table below says by how much.

The far south

Aswan and, 280 km further on, Abu Simbel — close enough to Sudan that the temples were cut apart and lifted 65 metres in the 1960s to escape the rising lake behind the High Dam. The hottest readings on this page are here.

The Western Desert

West of the river is the Sahara, punctuated by oases. Siwa is close to the Libyan border and was isolated enough to keep its own Berber language. The White Desert is chalk eroded into shapes. Both are a long drive from anywhere, which is the point of them.

Sinai and the Red Sea

The peninsula between Africa and Asia: mountains, Saint Catherine’s monastery at the foot of one of them, and reef coast at Dahab. Saint Catherine sits high enough to be genuinely cold in winter — the only place on this page where that is true.

Heat is the whole planning problem

What is it like in…?

Egypt’s sites are spread across nine degrees of latitude and the difference is not small. Pick a month and the map and table below both move to it.

What these numbers are not. They are long-run monthly averages modelled by NASA’s POWER climatology from the MERRA-2 reanalysis — not weather-station readings, and emphatically not a forecast. They answer “what is August usually like at Abu Simbel”. They cannot tell you anything about the day you actually travel, and a single hot week will not appear in them at all.

The headline figure is the monthly mean, not a daily high. An earlier version of this page labelled POWER’s T2M_MAX as the mean daily maximum. It is not — in climatology mode it is the hottest hour ever recorded in that month across the whole series, which for Giza in January is 29 °C against a typical daily high near 19. Those extremes are still shown, in the panel for each site, labelled as extremes. Expect a real day to run several degrees above the mean by afternoon and several below it overnight.

Why this is here and not a 3D flythrough. A cinematic aerial tour of Egypt would have to be either footage somebody shot, which we do not have, or imagery generated by a model, which would be invented terrain sitting next to measured figures on the same page. So this is the honest version of the same information: the actual ground elevation at each site’s own coordinates, from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission’s 30-metre survey. Every bar is a radar measurement of real land.

Where all of this came from

Sources, and what they cannot tell you.

No site is described in prose here, and that is deliberate. There is no sourced, licence-clear dataset of what Karnak feels like at dawn. Writing that would mean inventing atmosphere and setting it beside measured temperatures, where a reader would reasonably take it to be equally sourced. So each place shows what the data holds — where it is, how high, how hot each month, whether UNESCO lists it — and stops there.

Nothing here is a recommendation. “The best time to visit” depends on your tolerance for heat, your budget and how much you mind crowds, none of which are in any dataset. The measurement is shown; the judgement is yours.

The list of sites is ours. These — places were chosen as the ones a first-time visitor is realistically choosing between, which is an editorial call, not a ranking derived from data. Egypt has hundreds of significant sites; the gazetteer holds every one OpenStreetMap has mapped.
